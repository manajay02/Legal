K.M.G.H KULATUNGA, J.
1. The Petitioner had preferred this application for bail under Section 83(2)
of the Poison, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance as amended by
Act . The Petitioner is seeking bail for her husband,
Samaraweera Arachchige Jeewan Perera (Accused). Mr. Sumith

--- Page 3 ---
Senanayake, P.C., supporting this application sought bail on the
following grounds which he submits as being exceptional.
i. Delay of all most three and half years,
ii. No previous convictions.
2. Mr. Senanayake, P.C., submitted that the Accused was arrested on
18.04.2019 and is in remand up until now. His main contention is that
the trial has not concluded within a reasonable time and several dates
have been sought and the trial had been postponed at least on 06-07
occasions. The indictment is dated 02.07.2021 and the trial has
commenced on 06.10.2022. It was submitted that upon leading
evidence of witness No. 01, further trial had been postponed until today
and it is now fixed for further trial on 17.02.2025. Accordingly, it is
argued that this delay is excessive and oppressive and moves that the
Accused be granted bail.
3. The learned State Counsel objecting to this application submitted that
indictment has been filed without a delay and the pure quantity of
heroine involved in this offence is 3.749 kilogrammes. Whilst conceding
that the trial commenced on 06.12.2022 and the matter was postponed
for cross-examination of witness No.01. Thereafter upon the matter
being postponed on several occasions, it is now set for further trial for
17.02.2025. The position of the Respondents is that it is the Accused
and the defence that is primarily responsible for the postponements and
the delay. In support of this contention, the learned State Counsel
submitted that initially when it was for further trial, the defence had
moved for several dates in order to tender certain CCTV footages despite
time being obtained the said recordings were not produced and then
has abandoned and withdrawn the said application. Then, on
26.02.2024, a postponement had also been sought due to a personal
ground of a defence Counsel. These facts are confirmed by the certified
copy of the proceeding tendered by the Petitioner to this Court when
the matter is taken up for inquiry.

--- Page 4 ---
4. Accordingly, it is obvious and apparent that the defence is responsible
for a greater part of the postponements and dates obtained between
06.10.2022 and today. There is no way in which one could attribute
this delay to the prosecution or the respondents. An Accused cannot
create an exceptional circumstance by the postponements which the
defence had obtained. Such lapse of time may have caused a delay but
it is not excessive or oppressive.
5. As regards the lapse time, what may be exceptional is not the per se
lapse of time, but the excessive or oppressive delay. As held in Attorney
General v. Ediriweera (S.C. Appeal No. 100/2005), Delay is always a
relative term and the question to be considered is not whether there was
mere explicable delay, as when there is a backlog of cases, but whether
there has been excessive or oppressive delay and this always depends
on the facts and circumstances of the case.
6. According to the provisions of Section 83(2), bail in respect of persons
charged with trafficking or possession of 10 grammes or over may be
considered only if the applicants satisfy Court of exceptional
circumstances. However, the provisions of Section 83 (2) as amended
by Act, No. , decrees that a person accused or suspected of
being in possession of 10 grammes or more, is required to be kept in
remand, unless such person satisfies this Court as to the existence of
circumstances that are exceptional.
7. No doubt, under the normal law during the pre-trial and pre-conviction
stages granting of bail is the rule and the refusal is the exception. This
is in accord with the presumption of innocence which prevails during
the pendency of the trial. However, when exceptional circumstances are
by statute decreed as being a necessary prerequisite, then the
legislature has intended that, persons accused of such offences be held
in remand pending the determination of such trials. Considering
Section 83(1) of the Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance

--- Page 5 ---
(prior to the amendment) in Labukola Ange Wisin Gedera Ashani
Dhanushshika v. OIC Divisional Crimes Investigation Unit
Panadura [CA/PHC/APN/04/2016- CAM 06.10.2016] it was opined
thus;
The Petitioners first point is that the suspect is in remand nearly
for two years. The intention of the Legislature is to keep in remand
any person who is suspected of or accused of possessing or
trafficking heroine until the conclusion of the case. The Section
83(1) of the Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance
express the intention of the Legislature. It is enacted by the
Parliament that No person suspected or accused of an offence
under section 54A or section 54B of this Ordinance shall be
released on bail, except by the High Court in exceptional
circumstances.
8. The provisions of Section 83 have been enacted with the intent and
purpose of containing and curbing the growing drug-related offences.
The Poisons, Opium and Dangerous Drugs Ordinance being a special
statute could certainly prevail and override the provisions of other more
general statutes. In relation to bail, Section 83 has incorporated
provisions which decrees in the mandatory form that bail should not
ordinarily and as a rule be granted in cases involving serious drug
offences under subsection (1) of Section 54A and Section 54B except in
exceptional circumstances. When a special statute contains specific
provisions for granting of bail such provisions cannot be ignored whilst
considering such bail applications.
9. It is now settled law that the time spent in remand due to the lack of
exceptional circumstance cannot by itself create a circumstance that is
exceptional which otherwise would not have been there. This, in effect,
is that the period of remand due to the absence of exceptional
circumstances, will not, by itself, amount to or be converted to an
exceptional circumstance. This was succinctly expounded by Justice

--- Page 6 ---
Ranjith Silva in CA (PHC) APN 64/2009 (decided on 07.08.2009) as
follows;
If an accused cannot assign exceptional circumstances he
will have to be kept on remand and when an accused had
been on remand for 03 years because he had no exceptional
circumstances will that by itself constitute exceptional
circumstances. If that is treated as an exceptional
circumstance, in my view it would be an anomaly because
the fact that there arent any exceptional circumstances
finally mature into exceptional circumstances. The fact that
he had no exceptional circumstances becomes a qualification
after 03 years. If that was the intention of the legislature,
the section itself would have stated the exceptional
circumstances should not be insisted after 03 years and
there is no such qualification, no such jurisdiction found in
the particular provision dealing with bail.
10. Now to consider if the accused in this matter is entitled to any benefit
or if there be any ground that may be considered in his favour to grant
bail. On the face of it, there is certainly a time lag of three years from
the date of arrest up until now. Is this undue, oppressive or an excessive
delay? Undue, oppressive and excessive delay are not absolute terms
but would be relative depending on the context and circumstances in
which it is considered. The delay in concluding the trial in the present
case is attributable to the defence as the trial was postponed on an
application made on behalf of the accused (as admitted by both
counsel). To my mind when prolonging of a trial or the delay of a trial
is wholly or partly due to the conduct of the accused himself, he cannot
use such delay to his own benefit in seeking bail. It is a delay for which
the accused himself is to blame. Accordingly, the submission of
excessive or oppressive delay in the present context is misconceived and
not acceptable. The trial is now scheduled for 17.02.2025. As such, in

--- Page 7 ---
the absence of any exceptional circumstance we see no lawful basis to
consider this application favourably.
11. In the light of the aforesaid, this Court is of the view the Petitioner
had failed to establish any circumstance that may be considered
exceptional. This Court is left with no option but to refuse and reject
this application. Accordingly, this application is refused and dismissed.
