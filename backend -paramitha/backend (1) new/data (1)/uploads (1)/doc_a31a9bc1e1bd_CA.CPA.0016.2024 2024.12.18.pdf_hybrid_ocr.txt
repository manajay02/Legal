Introduction
The 1st and 2nd Respondent-Petitioner-Petitioners (hereinafter referred to as the
Petitioners) have filed this revision application against the Petitioner-
Respondent-Respondent (hereinafter referred to as the Respondent), seeking to
revise the judgment delivered by the High Court Judge of Puttalam on 14th
December 2023. The High Court Judge had refused to revise the order made by
the learned Magistrate of Anamaduwa on 13th October 2022 in an application
instituted under Section 66(1)(b) of the Primary Courts Procedure Act No. (hereinafter referred to as the Primary Court Procedure Act).
Additionally, the Petitioners seek to have the aforementioned order of the
Magistrate be set aside.

Factual background
The Respondent initially filed a Petition on 7th February 2022 in the Magistrates
Court of Anamaduwa under Section 66(1)(b) of the Primary Courts Procedure
Act, which was subsequently amended on 8th March 2022. The learned
Presidents Counsel for the Petitioners alleged that the Respondent altered the
scope of the application in the amended Petition. However, as this matter was
not raised by the Petitioners in the original Court, I will not treat it as an issue
for determination by this Court.
Along with the information submitted to the Magistrates Court, the Respondent
also filed several documents to support his contention1.
In response, the 1st Petitioner submitted an affidavit along with several
documents to establish his possession2.
The Respondent filed a counter-affidavit and submitted documents to challenge
the Petitioners claim of possession3.
Analysis
It is undisputed that the subject matter of this application is the Bethel Church
located in Serukale, Siyambalagaswewa, Anamaduwa.
The Respondent claims to be the President, Chief Priest, and Head of the
Administrative Board of Directors of the Assembly of God of Ceylon,
established under Act No. . He asserts that approximately 45 pastors
serve under him, including the 1st and 2nd Petitioners. The 1st Petitioner serves as
a pastor at Bethel Church, located in Serukale, Siyabalagaswewa, Anamaduwa,
which was constructed using funds contributed by the devotees of the said
Chapter. The Respondent further states that the Church was built on land
purchased from its permit holder, Nirosha Priyangani. According to the
Respondent, the Assembly of God of Ceylon is the rightful owner and
custodian of the Church.

According to the Petitioners, the 1st Petitioner obtained membership and a license

The Respondent alleges that, following their resignation, the Petitioners forcibly

The Respondent contended that the Church is not a residential place but a place
where people gather for prayers. The Petitioners have not claimed that they
reside in the Church. However, it is important to note that to possess a building,
one does not necessarily need to reside in it. Conducting religious prayers and
similar activities can constitute physical possession. In the case of Jayasinghe
and others v. Loku Bandara13, His Lordship Samayawardhane J. held that actual
possession does not require uninterrupted physical presence throughout the day.
The Respondents contention is that he had constructive possession of the
Church. However, it is important to note that this possession was through the
Petitioners, the rival party.
His Lordship Samayawardhane J., sitting in the Court of Appeal (as His Lordship
then was) in the aforementioned case of Jayasinghe and others v. Loku
Bandara14, concluded that in an application under Section 66 of the Primary
Courts Procedure Act, if the dispute is between the licensee and the licensor, the
licensees possession cannot be considered as the possession of the licensor.
There is no allegation by the Respondent that he was forcibly evicted from the
Church. His contention is that the Assembly of God of Ceylon had continuous
possession from the inception of the Bethel Church.

In the case of Iqbal v. Majedudeen and Others15, it was stated that the law
recognizes two types of possession.
i. When a person has direct physical control over a thing at a given time,
he is said to have actual possession of it.
ii. A person has constructive possession when he, though not in actual
possession, has both the power and the intention at a given time to
exercise dominium or control over a thing either directly or through
another person.
In my view, the Petitioners' involvement in religious activities as members of the
Assembly of God of Ceylon is somewhat akin to an activity carried out under a
license granted by another, acting as an agent. Once the license is revoked, the
right to conduct activities under that license ceases to exist; however, the
possessory rights would not be extinguished.
In an application under Section 66 of the Primary Courts Procedure Act, the
Magistrate is not required to investigate title. However, in the case of
Shanmugasundara Kurukkal Sri Skandarajah Kurukkal v. Ramalingham
Ranarajah and Sixteen Others16 (C.A.), His Lordship A. H. M. D. Nawaz J., (as
His Lordship then was), observed as follows:
The Magistrate is not required to investigate the title of the disputed land or the
rights of the administrative body. In fact, he can use the evidence of title merely
to guide and aid his mind in coming to a decision upon the question of
possession, but he is precluded from deciding questions of title alone.
According to the Grant issued by the State, the subject land belongs to Nirosha
Priyangani, and therefore, she should be considered the owner of the building
standing on it as well. The dominion of a property is naturally vested in its true
owner. In this case, the true owner, Nirosha Priyangani, has affirmed in an
affidavit that she authorized the 1st Petitioner to build on her land and to maintain
the Church within the premises.

However, as I have already stated above, the primary consideration in an
application of this nature is not the title, but rather the actual physical possession.
Based on the analysis of the facts outlined in this judgment, I am of the view that
the Petitioners were in physical possession of the Bethel Church on the date the
information was filed, and that the Respondent had not been dispossessed within
the two months immediately preceding the filing of the information.
Breach of the peace
One of the arguments advanced by the Petitioners (Respondents in the
Magistrates Court) was that there was no breach of the peace to invoke the
jurisdiction of the Magistrates Court under Section 66(1) of the Primary Courts
Procedure Act. As it was held in the case of Velupillai and others v. Sivanathan17
and followed by several other decisions, including Punchi Nona v. Padumasena
and others18, when information is filed under Section 66(1)(a) of the Act, the
formation of the opinion as to whether a breach of the peace is threatened or
likely is left to the Police Officer inquiring into the dispute. The Magistrate is
not required to inquire whether a breach of peace is threatened or likely, as
jurisdiction is vested in the Magistrate to inquire into and make a determination
on the dispute.
However, when an information is filed under Section 66(1)(b), it is incumbent
upon the Magistrate to first satisfy himself as to whether there is a threat or
likelihood of a breach of the peace.
Nevertheless, in the recent case of Jayasinghe and others v. Loku Bandara19
(C.A.), His Lordship Samayawardhane J., having scrutinized Section 66(1)(a)
and (b) of the Primary Courts Procedure Act, the repealed Section 62 of the
Administration of Justice Law, and the corresponding Section 145 of the Indian
Code of Criminal Procedure, held that: When first information is filed under
Section 66 (1) of the Primary Couts Procedure Act, irrespective of whether the
first information was filed by the Police or a party to the dispute, the Magistrate

is automatically vested with jurisdiction under Section 66(2) to inquire into and
determine the matter. There is no further requirement for the Magistrate to
ascertain whether the breach of the peace is threatened or likely.
In any event, I observe that the learned Magistrate, in her impugned order, has
carefully analysed the question of whether there was a threatened breach of the
peace. The learned Magistrate has, inter alia, referred to a letter written to the
Assembly of God of Ceylon by the Petitioner, in which it is stated that the
devotees of the Bethel Church have divided into two factions, thereby creating a
potential breach of the peace.
The Respondent has produced a letter signed by several individuals stating that
the devotees are agitated due to the conduct of the 1st Petitioner, and there is an
imminent threat of a breach of the peace20. Additionally, an agent of the
Respondent has sent a letter to the Officer in Charge of the Pallama Police
Station, seeking police assistance to establish their title21.
Even when the evidence presented to the learned Magistrate is examined, this
Court observes that two factions have submitted affidavits in support of the
Petitioner and the Respondent, which contradict each other.
Consequently, based on the available evidence, I am satisfied that there was a
potential threat of a breach of the peace, as correctly concluded by the learned
Magistrate and upheld by the learned High Court Judge.

(iii) The learned High Court Judge erred in law and thereby caused grave
miscarriage of justice on the Petitioners by failing to analyse the purported
dispossession by the Petitioners.
I will begin by considering a judgment delivered by this Court on a similar issue.
In the case of Shanmugasundara Kurukkal Sri Skandarajah Kurukkal v.
Ramalingham Ranarajah and Sixteen others22 (C.A.), His Lordship Nawaz J.
considered a dispute between a Kurukkal (or Poosari) and the administrative
body of a temple. His Lordship examined whether the possession of the Kurukkal
satisfied the requirements of possession under Section 68.
According to the facts of the case, the administrative body of the temple claimed
to have some kind of right to summarily remove a priest. His Lordship
considered what gave them this power? Did they appoint him as a priest? If so,
did it entitle them to oust him in the manner in which he was removed?
It was an admitted fact that the members of the administrative body broke open
the closed door of the temple, to which the Kurukkal had the keys, secured it
with new locks, and took possession from him.
The learned Magistrate ordered that the Kurukkal, who had been dispossessed,
be restored to possession.
In the revision application filed against the said order, the learned High Court
Judge held that since the temple and its properties are always vested in the
administrative body of the temple, the body has the power to remove a priest at
any time. It was further held that merely having custody of the keys did not
amount to possession within the meaning of Section 68(3) of the Primary Courts
Procedure Act. The Kurukkal only held ritual rights to conduct the pooja and
should vindicate those rights through an action in the District Court.
Consequently, the learned High Court Judge overturned the decision of the
learned Magistrate.

His Lordship observed that the learned High Court Judge overturned the decision
of the learned Primary Court Judge on the grounds that the possession of the
Kurukkal could not be equated to exclusive possession, similar to the possession
of a Bank Manager or a Hotel Manager.
In analysing the judgment of the learned High Court Judge, His Lordship
considered several judgments in which the elements of possession were
discussed. In the case Iqbal v. Majedudeen and others23, His Lordship U.D.Z.
Gunawardana J. observed that the words forcibly dispossessed in Section 68(3)
of the Primary Courts Procedure Act mean that dispossession took place against
the will of the person entitled to possess, and without the authority of the law.
In the course of the judgment, His Lordship Nawaz J. observed as follows:
In fact, there is no evidence, so to speak, to establish that the Respondent has
actual possession of this temple. It was the Appellant who had been in
possession of the temple premises by virtue of the fact that his presence therein
was necessitated by his functions and the fact that the key to the temple had
been in his custody at the relevant time of dispossession connotes actual
possession of the temple premises on the part of the Appellant. (Emphasis
added)
Finally, His Lordship Nawaz J., held that actual possession should be real and
tangible, meaning there should be effective occupation and control of the
property.
His Lordship concluded that the administrative body had ousted the Kurukkal,
who had been in possession of the temple premises, within a period of two
months preceding the date the information was filed.
The facts of the above case are almost identical to the case at hand. The parties
are not at variance that the Petitioners conducted the service of the Bethel Church
as Pastors.

Whether there are exceptional circumstances
The primary consideration before examining the merits of a revision application
is whether exceptional circumstances exist that justify the Courts intervention,
as this serves as the gateway to relief.
The Petitioners have sought the exercise of this Courts revisionary jurisdiction
with respect to both the order of the learned High Court Judge and the order of
the learned Magistrate.
In the case of Hotel Galaxy (Pvt) Ltd and others v. Mercantile Hotels
Management Ltd24, Sharvananda C.J. observed that:
It is settled law that the exercise of revisionary powers of the Appellate Court
is confined to cases in which exceptional circumstances exist warranting its
intervention.
In the case of Divisional Secretary Kalutara v. Kalupahana Mestrige Jayathissa
and others25, the Supreme Court observed that:
It must be noted that the Respondents had invoked revisionary jurisdiction of
this court, which is discretionary remedy. Thus, if relief to be granted, the party
seeking the relief has to establish that, not only the impugned Order is illegal,
but also the nature of the illegality is such, that it shocks the conscience of the
court.
Based on the reasons outlined above, it is my considered view that the Petitioners
have demonstrated that the impugned orders of the learned Magistrate and the
learned High Court Judge are manifestly erroneous and, therefore, shock the
conscience of this Court, warranting intervention.

Conclusion
In light of the above analysis, I hold that the Respondent cannot be considered
as having had constructive possession, and that the Petitioners were in
continuous actual physical possession prior to and at the time of filing the
information in the Magistrates Court. Furthermore, there was no dispossession
of either party within two months of the filing of the information.
For the foregoing reasons, I hold that the learned High Court Judge erred in
upholding the order of the learned Magistrate by misinterpreting the pertinent
statutory provisions and the relevant principles of constructive possession.
After considering all the submitted materials and the arguments of both Counsel,
this Court concludes that both the impugned orders of the learned Magistrate and
the learned High Court Judge cannot stand. Consequently, I allow the application
and, acting in revision, set aside the order of the learned High Court Judge dated
14th December 2023, as well as the order of the learned Magistrate dated 13th
October 2022.
I confirm the Petitioners' possession and direct that they be restored to possession
immediately, if they have been evicted.
Application allowed, subject to a cost of Rs. 50,000/-.
The Registrar of this Court is directed to communicate to the High Court of
